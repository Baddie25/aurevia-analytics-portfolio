# Project 2 — Market Entry Strategy
## Market Screening Framework

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the analytical framework Aurevia Group will use to
compare eligible international markets after the market-universe and
eligibility stage.

The framework translates the Project 2 executive question into a structured
market-screening process.

The objective is not to identify the most attractive country in isolation.
The objective is to identify which markets warrant deeper investment
analysis based on commercial opportunity, economic conditions, consumer
potential, competitive environment, strategic fit, and entry feasibility.

Market screening is a prioritization exercise.

It is not the final investment decision.

---

## 2. Executive Question

The screening analysis supports the question:

> **Which eligible markets offer the strongest combination of commercial
> opportunity, consumer potential, strategic fit, and manageable entry risk
> for Aurevia, and therefore deserve deeper investment analysis?**

The final investment decision remains separate:

> **Should Aurevia enter, delay entry, or not enter a shortlisted market?**

A market can therefore perform strongly during screening and still fail the
subsequent investment case.

---

## 3. Analytical Sequence

The market-selection process follows this sequence:

```text
Candidate Universe
        ↓
Eligibility Assessment
        ↓
Eligible Markets
        ↓
Evidence Collection
        ↓
Market Screening
        ↓
Shortlist
        ↓
Deep Market Analysis
        ↓
Entry Economics
        ↓
Risk & Scenario Analysis
        ↓
Investment Decision
```

The screening stage must not bypass eligibility.

The investment stage must not be replaced by the screening result.

---

## 4. Screening Dimensions

Eligible markets will be assessed across six dimensions:

1. Market Opportunity
2. Economic Environment
3. Consumer Potential
4. Competitive Environment
5. Strategic Fit
6. Entry Feasibility and Risk

The dimensions are deliberately broad enough to capture the strategic decision while remaining sufficiently specific to support reproducible analysis.

---

## 5. Dimension 1 — Market Opportunity

### Objective

Determine whether the market contains a sufficiently attractive commercial opportunity for Aurevia's relevant product categories.

### Core indicators

#### 5.1 Relevant market size

Measure the size of the relevant consumer category or categories where credible data is available.

Preferred measures include:

- Category revenue
- Category retail sales
- Category consumption value
- Relevant market volume

The relevant category must be defined explicitly.

National consumer spending or GDP must not be presented as a substitute for category-level market size.

#### 5.2 Market growth

Assess historical and, where credible, forecast growth.

Preferred measures include:

- Historical CAGR
- Year-over-year growth
- Published market-growth forecasts

Growth periods must be recorded.

Historical growth and forecast growth must not be combined without clear labelling.

#### 5.3 Addressable consumer base

Assess the size and characteristics of the consumer population relevant to Aurevia's products.

Potential indicators include:

- Relevant population
- Urban population
- Household numbers
- Relevant age groups
- Income-qualified consumers

The selected indicator must match the product category being assessed.

#### 5.4 Market development

Assess whether the category appears mature, developing, or structurally underpenetrated.

Potential evidence includes:

- Category penetration
- Per-capita consumption
- E-commerce penetration
- Retail development
- Premiumization or value-seeking trends

These indicators are contextual rather than standalone measures of attractiveness.

### Direction of preference

Generally:

- Larger relevant market → positive
- Faster sustainable growth → positive
- Larger addressable consumer base → positive
- Evidence of structural category development → positive

However, market size without attainable access does not establish an attractive investment opportunity.

---

## 6. Dimension 2 — Economic Environment

### Objective

Assess whether the broader economic environment is supportive of consumer demand and investment.

### Core indicators

#### 6.1 GDP per capita

Used as a broad indicator of economic development and purchasing power.

Where possible, the analysis should distinguish between:

- Current US-dollar measures
- Purchasing-power-adjusted measures

These should not be treated as interchangeable.

#### 6.2 Real GDP growth

Assess recent economic growth and available forward-looking expectations.

#### 6.3 Inflation

Assess recent inflation and inflation stability.

Persistent high inflation can affect:

- Consumer purchasing power
- Pricing
- Input costs
- Working capital
- Forecast reliability

#### 6.4 Currency environment

Assess relevant currency considerations for Aurevia.

Potential indicators include:

- Historical exchange-rate movement
- Currency volatility
- Recent depreciation/appreciation
- Availability of relevant FX data

Currency conditions will become more important during the investment modelling stage.

#### 6.5 Macroeconomic stability

Consider evidence relating to:

- External vulnerability
- Fiscal or monetary conditions
- Economic volatility
- Material macroeconomic shocks

No single macroeconomic indicator should determine market selection.

### Direction of preference

Generally:

- Higher purchasing power → positive
- Sustainable economic growth → positive
- Lower and more stable inflation → positive
- Lower material FX instability → positive
- Greater macroeconomic stability → positive

Economic attractiveness must be considered alongside the specific consumer opportunity.

---

## 7. Dimension 3 — Consumer Potential

### Objective

Assess whether consumers in the market have characteristics that support Aurevia's relevant products and channels.

### Core indicators

#### 7.1 Population scale

Assess total population as contextual market potential.

Population alone is not sufficient evidence of demand.

#### 7.2 Income and purchasing power

Where available, assess:

- Disposable income
- Household income
- Income per capita
- Relevant consumer expenditure

#### 7.3 Urbanization and consumer concentration

Assess:

- Urban population
- Major metropolitan markets
- Geographic concentration of potential customers

This may influence distribution economics and launch strategy.

#### 7.4 Demographic fit

Assess demographics relevant to the products being considered.

Potential indicators include:

- Age structure
- Household composition
- Population growth
- Relevant consumer segments

#### 7.5 Consumer-channel development

Where relevant, assess:

- E-commerce adoption
- Digital consumer penetration
- Modern retail development
- Specialty retail development

### Direction of preference

Generally:

- Larger relevant consumer base → positive
- Higher purchasing power → positive
- Stronger relevant demographic fit → positive
- Greater channel accessibility → positive

Indicators must be interpreted in relation to Aurevia's actual product and channel strategy.

---

## 8. Dimension 4 — Competitive Environment

### Objective

Determine whether Aurevia could plausibly establish a position in the market and understand the intensity of existing competition.

### Core indicators

#### 8.1 Competitive intensity

Assess:

- Number and strength of relevant competitors
- Presence of major multinational brands
- Strength of local competitors
- Category fragmentation or concentration

#### 8.2 Market structure

Where evidence permits, assess:

- Leading companies
- Approximate market shares
- Concentration
- Channel concentration

Market-share figures must identify their source, period, category definition, and geographic coverage.

#### 8.3 Competitor positioning

Assess competitor positioning across dimensions such as:

- Price
- Product breadth
- Premium/value positioning
- Distribution
- Brand strength
- Digital presence

#### 8.4 Competitive barriers

Identify barriers that may make market entry difficult.

Potential barriers include:

- Strong incumbent relationships
- Exclusive distribution
- Brand loyalty
- High customer acquisition costs
- Regulatory barriers
- Scale advantages
- Local manufacturing advantages

### Direction of preference

There is no universally correct direction.

A large competitive market may indicate both:

- Strong existing demand, and
- Greater difficulty capturing market share.

Therefore competitive evidence will be interpreted rather than mechanically scored as "more competition = worse."

The final assessment must explain whether competition represents:

- Market validation
- Entry difficulty
- An opportunity for differentiation
- Or some combination of these.

---

## 9. Dimension 5 — Strategic Fit

### Objective

Assess how naturally the opportunity fits Aurevia's existing business, capabilities, products, channels, and geographic position.

Strategic fit is Aurevia-specific and therefore cannot be derived entirely from public country-level statistics.

### Core indicators

#### 9.1 Product-category fit

Identify which Aurevia divisions and products have the strongest plausible fit with the market.

Potential mapping:

- Personal Care
- Home & Lifestyle
- Wellness
- Everyday Essentials

Product-level fit should be based on market evidence rather than assumed consumer similarity.

#### 9.2 Channel fit

Assess compatibility with Aurevia's existing commercial channels:

- Retail
- E-commerce
- Distributors
- Direct-to-Consumer
- Specialty

The assessment should identify which channels appear most feasible and why.

#### 9.3 Capability fit

Assess whether Aurevia's existing capabilities could support entry.

Relevant considerations include:

- Product portfolio
- Distribution capability
- Digital capability
- Commercial model
- Supply-chain capabilities
- Brand positioning

#### 9.4 Geographic and strategic fit

Consider:

- Existing Aurevia geographic footprint
- Potential regional expansion logic
- Supply-chain adjacency
- Market diversification
- Potential strategic learning or capability development

Geographic proximity alone does not establish strategic fit.

### Direction of preference

Generally:

- Stronger product fit → positive
- Stronger channel compatibility → positive
- Greater capability reuse → positive
- More credible regional or strategic rationale → positive

Strategic fit must be explicitly tied to Aurevia rather than inferred from country attractiveness alone.

---

## 10. Dimension 6 — Entry Feasibility and Risk

### Objective

Assess whether Aurevia could realistically enter and operate in the market under an acceptable risk profile.

### Core indicators

#### 10.1 Regulatory complexity

Assess:

- Product registration
- Certification
- Labelling requirements
- Import requirements
- Product-specific restrictions
- Relevant investment or ownership rules

#### 10.2 Market-access barriers

Assess:

- Tariffs
- Customs requirements
- Import procedures
- Distribution restrictions
- Local partner requirements

#### 10.3 Operating environment

Consider:

- Logistics infrastructure
- Distribution capability
- Digital infrastructure
- Availability of commercial partners
- Supply-chain reliability

#### 10.4 Political and institutional risk

Assess material evidence relating to:

- Political stability
- Institutional environment
- Policy uncertainty
- Government effectiveness
- Country-specific operating risks

#### 10.5 Financial risk

Consider:

- Currency exposure
- Inflation
- Repatriation considerations
- Financing environment
- Potential working-capital pressure

These factors will be modelled more explicitly during investment analysis.

### Direction of preference

Generally:

- Lower regulatory friction → positive
- Lower market-access barriers → positive
- Stronger operating infrastructure → positive
- Lower material political/institutional risk → positive
- Lower financial volatility → positive

Risk assessment must distinguish between:

- Probability
- Potential impact
- Mitigating actions
- Residual risk

## 11. Indicator Hierarchy

Not all indicators will have equal analytical importance.

The framework therefore uses three levels.

### Level 1 — Core indicators

Indicators expected to be available and directly relevant to the screening decision.

Examples:

- Relevant market size
- Relevant market growth
- GDP per capita
- Inflation
- Population
- Consumer purchasing-power indicators
- Competitive structure
- Regulatory feasibility

### Level 2 — Supporting indicators

Indicators that strengthen interpretation where reliable evidence exists.

Examples:

- Urbanization
- E-commerce penetration
- Category penetration
- Currency volatility
- Retail development
- Distribution infrastructure

### Level 3 — Contextual evidence

Qualitative or supplementary evidence used to explain the quantitative results.

Examples:

- Consumer trends
- Competitor announcements
- Local market developments
- Regulatory developments
- Strategic observations

Level 3 evidence must not be presented as if it were a directly comparable quantitative metric.

---

## 12. Data Comparability Rules

Comparability is a prerequisite for quantitative screening.

Before combining or comparing an indicator, the analysis must verify:

- Same or compatible definition
- Same unit
- Same geographic coverage
- Comparable observation period
- Comparable population or category scope
- Consistent treatment of nominal versus real values

Where definitions differ materially, the indicators will remain separate.

A more precise-looking number is not automatically a better number.

---

## 13. Time-Period Rules

The Project 2 analysis will distinguish between:

### Historical evidence

Used to understand what has happened.

Where practical, the analysis should examine multiple years rather than a single observation.

### Current evidence

Used to establish the market's present position.

### Forecast evidence

Used to understand potential future development.

Forecasts must be labelled as forecasts and attributed to their source.

### Aurevia assumptions

Used only for company-specific modelling where external evidence cannot establish the required value.

These assumptions must be maintained in the assumption register.

Historical data, forecasts, and Aurevia assumptions must not be blended without clear labelling.

---

## 14. Missing Data Treatment

Missing data will not automatically receive a zero.

The analytical treatment will distinguish among:

- Data unavailable
- Data not applicable
- Data available but not comparable
- Data available from lower-quality sources
- Data available with material methodological limitations

If a core indicator cannot be established reliably, this limitation must be visible in the screening assessment.

No market will receive an artificially favourable or unfavourable score because of missing information.

---

## 15. Screening Method

The first screening pass will use an evidence-led market profile rather than immediately relying on a single composite score.

For each market, the analysis will produce:

1. Core quantitative indicators
2. Supporting indicators
3. Qualitative evidence
4. Evidence-quality assessment
5. Key opportunities
6. Key constraints
7. Material uncertainties
8. Initial strategic interpretation

Only after this evidence profile has been reviewed will a comparative scoring or ranking method be considered.

If a composite score is introduced, it must:

- Use documented weights
- Have a clear business rationale
- Use comparable indicators
- Make normalization transparent
- Preserve evidence-quality limitations
- Include sensitivity testing
- Never determine the investment decision automatically

The screening score, if used, will be a prioritization aid rather than a substitute for strategic judgement.

---

## 16. Preliminary Shortlisting Logic

The screening stage will identify approximately three to four markets for deeper investigation.

The shortlist should represent markets that combine:

- Meaningful commercial opportunity
- Credible consumer potential
- Reasonable economic conditions
- Plausible competitive positioning
- Strong or defensible Aurevia strategic fit
- Manageable entry barriers and risk
- Sufficient evidence for investment modelling

The shortlist must not simply consist of the four markets with the highest numerical values.

Qualitative evidence and material risks may justify selecting or excluding a market despite its relative quantitative position.

The shortlist rationale must be documented.

---

## 17. Red-Flag Review

Before finalizing the shortlist, every market must undergo a red-flag review.

Potential red flags include:

- Major regulatory uncertainty
- Severe market-access barriers
- Extreme macroeconomic instability
- Significant currency risk
- Strong incumbent control of relevant channels
- Weak evidence of relevant product demand
- Inability to establish credible entry economics
- Material supply or distribution constraints
- Major strategic mismatch with Aurevia

A red flag does not automatically eliminate a market.

It requires explicit assessment of:

```text
Risk
  ↓
Probability
  ↓
Potential Impact
  ↓
Mitigation
  ↓
Residual Risk
```

## 19. Screening-to-Investment Transition

Markets selected for the shortlist will move into deeper analysis.

The next stage will investigate:

### Market

- TAM
- SAM
- SOM
- Category structure
- Consumer segments
- Growth outlook

### Competition

- Major competitors
- Market shares where available
- Positioning
- Pricing
- Distribution
- Competitive advantages

### Aurevia strategy

- Product-market fit
- Channel strategy
- Entry mode
- Localization
- Partner requirements

### Economics

- Revenue assumptions
- Market-share assumptions
- Pricing
- Costs
- Investment
- Working capital
- Cash flows
- Break-even
- NPV
- IRR

### Risk

- Downside scenario
- Base scenario
- Upside scenario
- Sensitivity analysis
- Material strategic and operational risks

The shortlist therefore represents a transition from:

> **"This market deserves investigation."**

to:

> **"This market may justify investment."**

Those statements must not be treated as equivalent.

## 21. Quality Gate

The screening framework is complete only when:

- The six screening dimensions are documented.
- Core and supporting indicators are defined.
- Indicator definitions are explicit.
- Direction of preference is documented where meaningful.
- Data comparability rules are established.
- Historical, current, forecast, and assumed values are distinguished.
- Missing-data treatment is documented.
- Competitive intensity is interpreted rather than mechanically ranked.
- Strategic fit is assessed specifically for Aurevia.
- Entry risk is assessed separately from commercial opportunity.
- Red flags are explicitly reviewed.
- The shortlist is supported by evidence.
- No market is selected solely because of a composite score.
- The screening result remains separate from the final investment decision.

---

## 22. Governing Principle

The market-screening framework exists to answer:

> **Which markets deserve deeper analysis?**

It does not answer:

> **Should Aurevia invest?**

That question belongs to the investment case.

The quality of Project 2 depends on maintaining this distinction.

A market can be large but inaccessible.

A market can be fast-growing but unprofitable.

A market can be profitable but strategically unsuitable.

A market can be risky but attractive if the risk is measurable and manageable.

The purpose of screening is therefore not to find the market with the best headline statistics.

It is to identify the markets where the evidence suggests that deeper strategic and financial analysis is most likely to produce a credible investment decision.
